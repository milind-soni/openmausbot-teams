# Attention to Sale

Provenance: built by Lyle (OpenMausBot Marketing) on 2026-11-24 from a research pass by three researchers covering attention science, sales and advertising effectiveness, and real breakout case studies. Every claim carries a strength label. Platform and vendor numbers are marked as such, because they sell the thing they measure. Sources are at the end.

## The one idea

Nobody watches ads. People watch whatever seems worth their next second, and they skip anything that looks like it wants something from them. So the job comes in this order:

1. Show something real, specific, and alive in the first frame.
2. Earn the next three seconds with an open question or a feeling.
3. Only then, ask.

Most bad marketing reverses this. It opens with the ask (title, logo, offer, badge) and hopes the feeling shows up later. It never does, because the viewer is already gone.

## 1. What the evidence says

### Strong (independent research, large samples, or replicated)

- **The decision is fast.** Facebook IQ (platform study) puts average mobile feed time per item at 1.7 seconds. People could recall content after 0.25 seconds of exposure. The first frame has to work on its own, with the sound off.
- **Anything that looks like an ad gets skipped, even useful things.** Nielsen Norman Group eye-tracking shows people ignore content that has ad-like placement, ad-like styling (boxed backgrounds, badges, fancy formatting, animation), or that sits near ads. On mobile, big standout images get mistaken for ads and scrolled past. Polish is not neutral: it signals "ad".
- **High-arousal emotion spreads, low-arousal emotion does not.** Berger and Milkman studied about 7,000 NYT articles. One standard deviation more awe raised the odds of making the most-emailed list by about 30%, and anger by 34%. Anxiety also drove sharing. Sadness reduced it. Awe, dread, anger, delight and humor travel. Wistful mood does not.
- **Free changes behavior beyond the math.** Shampanier, Mazar and Ariely ran real-purchase experiments: dropping a price from 1 cent to free pulls choice far more than any other one-cent change. This is the zero price effect.
- **Reviews matter most at the start.** Spiegel Research Center (Northwestern; retail products, not books) found 5 reviews make a purchase about 270% more likely than zero. The gains flatten after that. Purchase likelihood peaks around a 4.0 to 4.7 average and drops near a perfect 5.0, which reads as fake.
- **Concrete beats abstract for memory.** Paivio's dual coding research shows concrete material is recalled roughly 2 to 1 over abstract. The effect is real but not universal. "A woman lifts a car off a stranger" sticks. "An epic battle between light and darkness" does not.

### Good (large commercial datasets, not independently replicated)

- **Emotion builds long-term results.** In the IPA databank of about 1,000 campaigns, Binet and Field found emotional campaigns roughly twice as likely to report top profit growth as rational ones. Broad reach beat narrow targeting. The best split was about 60% brand building and 40% direct activation, and the two together beat either alone.
- **Characters, place and story make ads memorable. Abstract claims make them click-bait.** System1 and Orlando Wood found that ads with people, a sense of place, a scene and a story lift brand recall (1.6x in an Amazon study). Flat, direct "reason why" ads drive immediate clicks (2x detail-page visits) but are forgotten. Wood argues modern ads have drifted flat, abstract and peopleless. That drift is a big part of why ads feel corny.
- **Growth means reaching people who are not looking.** Ehrenberg-Bass (Byron Sharp) finds most buyers of anything are light buyers, and growth comes from reaching many of them. To be chosen you must come to mind (mental availability) and be easy to find (physical availability). Distinctive, consistent assets build the first.
- **About 2.5 seconds of real attention is where memory starts to form.** Amplified Intelligence (vendor) reports that about 85% of paid ads never get there. Distinctive brand assets may register in about 1.5 seconds (with VCCP, from a secondary summary).
- **Faces pull attention, but gaze steers it.** Instagram photos with faces got 38% more likes and 32% more comments (Bakhshi et al., CHI 2014; about 1 million images; correlational). In banner ads, a face staring at the viewer grabs attention for itself. A face looking toward the product sends attention to the product (Sajjacholapunt and Ball, 2014).

### Weak or practitioner-level (useful defaults, test before trusting)

- **Hook rate** is 3-second views divided by impressions. Vendor averages are about 25 to 30%, with strong ads at 40 to 50%.
- **Hold rate** is roughly 6-second views divided by 3-second views. A rough guide is 40 to 50% as fine and under 30% as weak.
- **Native, creator-style content beats studio polish.** The evidence is mostly TikTok-commissioned perception studies, not outcome tests.
- **Question hooks lift clicks but can lower conversion.** The tests are mixed and confounded.
- **Creative fatigue:** refresh when hook rate or CTR drops about 25% from its own baseline. This is a heuristic.

## 2. How unknowns actually break out

Across about a dozen cases (It Ends With Us, The Martian, Wool, Dungeon Crawler Carl, The Housemaid, Fifty Shades, Alchemised, Liquid Death, Dollar Shave Club, the Stanley Quencher, Duolingo, Purple):

- **The spark was almost never an ad.** It was a real person's reaction, or one concrete moment you could show someone. Readers filmed themselves crying over a Hoover book, and weekly sales went from under 100 to about 17,000. A Quencher survived a car fire on camera. A $1,500 fake commercial for canned water, shot before the product existed, made people ask "is this real?"
- **Ads amplified a spark that already existed.** Atria and Stanley won by noticing the organic moment and pouring fuel on it. They did not manufacture one.
- **There was a cheap or free way in**: 99 cents, a free serial, a free first book, an audiobook.
- **There was usually a community first**: a serial site, fan fiction, a reader group, a newsletter.
- **Retail follows proof.** Barnes & Noble built BookTok tables and bought deeper after the sales showed up. Costco and Sam's Club, and the B&N endcap, are the result of demand, not the cause. You earn the table with velocity and readers talking.
- **It takes time.** Most breakouts took 2 to 4 years. A dormant book can still be lit later.
- **The one-line concept was clear.** "An astronaut stranded on Mars, solving it with science." "Shaves for a buck." If a stranger cannot repeat it after one look, it will not spread.

For books specifically, the reader's experience is the product people share: the feeling, the moment, the "I could not stop". The cover and the title are just how they find it afterward.

## 3. Why creative feels corny or template-made

Hold each piece against these. Each failure maps to a finding above.

- **It looks like an ad**, with badges, pill boxes, banners, logo-first or title-card openings, or polished poster lighting. That breaks the ad-avoidance finding. AI-poster aesthetics (glossy, symmetrical, over-lit, generic dramatic) are now their own ad cue, and people pattern-match them instantly.
- **It is abstract.** Mood words, taglines like "Darkness rises" or "She will stop at nothing", and vague stakes break the concreteness finding.
- **Nobody is in it, or the person is a mannequin.** No character, place or scene breaks the Wood and System1 finding. A posed figure staring at the camera adds nothing.
- **It sells the product, not the experience.** "Book 1 now available" is the ask. Where is the thing worth feeling?
- **The emotion is low-arousal**, pretty, moody and tasteful. Dread, awe, anger and laughter are what move people.
- **Everything comes from one mold**, so nothing is distinctive. Templates kill novelty and teach the viewer to skip you faster. Consistency belongs in one distinctive asset, not in a repeated layout.
- **It overpromises or lies.** It works once and then poisons reviews and trust.
- **It was judged on the wrong thing.** Specs, hashes and pixel checks confirm a file is clean. They do not tell you whether a stranger would stop. Always ask the stranger question first.

## 4. How to work (a method, not a template)

1. **Find the true moment.** Dig into the actual product for its most concrete, surprising, emotionally charged real thing. For a book, that is a scene, an image, a line of action, or a situation from the opening that a stranger instantly understands. If you cannot say it in one plain sentence, do not make anything yet.
2. **Name the feeling.** Pick one high-arousal emotion: awe, dread, anger, delight, or "wait, what?". Write it down. If the honest answer is "it looks cool", start over.
3. **Write the first frame before anything else.** Assume sound off, a phone at arm's length, and 1 second. The frame should show something happening or about to happen, ideally a person in a real place. Put no title card, logo, offer or badge first.
4. **Open a gap and leave it open.** Give enough that they care, then withhold the answer. Curiosity is an inverted U: too little information and nobody cares, too much and there is no reason to stay. For fiction, this is also how you avoid spoilers. Sell the question, never the answer.
5. **Make it native.** Ask whether a real person would post this. Use the format, framing and texture of what people choose to watch on that platform. A plain, real thing beats a slick fake one.
6. **Keep one distinctive asset and repeat it everywhere**, for example a color, a sound, a type treatment, a recurring character or a phrase. That is how the tenth impression builds on the first. Repeat the asset, never the layout.
7. **Ask last, and give the reason.** The offer goes at the end, said plainly. A free or very cheap offer needs a stated reason ("Book 1 is free this week so you can see if the series is for you"), because a deal that looks too good triggers suspicion of a hidden catch.
8. **Make 3 to 5 truly different concepts.** Each should differ in its moment and its feeling, not just in color or wording. Let the numbers choose.
9. **Prefer people over production.** A real reader's reaction, or real footage of the thing being tested or used, beats anything staged. Earn reactions honestly and never fake them.
10. **Earn proof before you pay for reach.** Get to about 5 honest reviews before paid ads. Ads magnify whatever is already there, including nothing.

## 5. Measuring (data picks the winner)

Track these in order. Each one tells you where the leak is.

| Metric | What it tells you | Fix it with |
|---|---|---|
| Hook rate (3-second views / impressions) | Did the first frame stop them? | A new moment, a new first frame |
| Hold rate (about 6-second views / 3-second views) | Did the gap hold them? | A sharper question, cut the slow part |
| CTR (clicks / impressions) | Did they want more? | A clearer ask, a better feeling-to-offer match |
| Landing conversion (downloads or buys / clicks) | Did the page keep the promise? | Cover, blurb, reviews, price, and a match with the ad |
| Read-through or repeat (book 2 sales / book 1 readers) | Was the product worth it? | The product itself, plus the end-of-book next step |

Test rules:

- Change one big variable at a time, a different moment or feeling first and small tweaks last.
- Run each variant to at least about 2,000 impressions before judging. BookBub's guidance is that $5 to $10 per test is usually enough.
- Judge against your own baseline, not vendor benchmarks.
- Keep a spend-vs-return log for every test: date, variant, spend, impressions, hook rate, CTR, conversions, and the decision you made.
- Refresh a winner when its hook rate or CTR drops about 25% from its own peak.
- Organic posts are free tests. A post that real people share or comment on is the best concept to put money behind.

## 6. Checked and not proven (do not state these as fact)

- No measured, published numbers exist for faces, motion or text overlays in short-form ads specifically.
- No head-to-head outcome test of lo-fi versus polished creative exists; the evidence is platform perception studies.
- No measured case exists of a paid ad that imitated a reader-reaction post. Panels report that labeled "sponsored" content underperforms on BookTok.
- No data was found on how many free ebook downloads are actually read; the reports are anecdotal and suggest many are not.
- BookBub's published ad tests report winners, not CTR lifts. One surprise: a plain background beat an enlarged cover background.
- Cialdini's hotel-towel social-norm effect (37% vs 50%) failed to replicate in two German hotel studies. Use social proof as a test, not a law.

## Hard limits (always)

- Tell the truth about the product. Never fake reviews, reactions, scarcity or urgency.
- For fiction, show no spoilers. Sell the opening situation and the question.
- Disclose AI assistance honestly where it applies, and skip communities and sites that ban it.
- Never spend, post, publish, or touch live accounts without the owner's explicit yes.
